Research Proposal Defense Questions: 25 Questions to Practise

A proposal defense question tests a decision in your planned study: why this problem, why this evidence, why this method, and how the project remains credible if conditions change. The exact format, timing and rules belong to your programme. Use its handbook and committee instructions as the authority.

This page is for the distinct task behind the query research proposal defense questions: build answer notes and practise follow-ups. For the process and outcomes, read what a research proposal defense is; for a complete presentation and mock-defence plan, use how to defend a research proposal.

Answer principle: do not defend every choice as perfect. Show a traceable chain: claim → evidence → design choice → realistic alternative → boundary or control.

What a panel may be testing

Official programme guidance shows why question sets vary. Wright State describes its proposal defense as an oral examination of the proposal's validity and the candidate's fitness to do the work. The University of Washington's Aeronautics & Astronautics guidance asks candidates to explain the basis of procedures, results to date and alternate strategies. Use these as examples of question logic, not as rules for every institution.

Question familyDecision being testedUseful evidence to have ready
Problem and contributionWhether the project is worth doingDefined problem, literature gap and bounded contribution

25 research proposal defense questions

1. Problem, gap and contribution

  1. What specific problem will this research help people understand or decide?
  2. What does the current literature explain, and what important uncertainty remains?
  3. Why is this research question narrow enough to answer within the project?
  4. What is the expected contribution: evidence, method, theory, practice or a combination?
  5. What would count as a result that challenges your starting assumption?

2. Research design and methods

  1. Why does this method fit the research question better than the strongest realistic alternative?
  2. What evidence will the design produce, and what claim can that evidence support?
  3. How will you select participants, cases, sources or data?
  4. How will you analyse the material and check that the interpretation is credible?
  5. What are the main threats to validity, reliability, transferability or reproducibility?

3. Ethics, data and risk

  1. Which people, groups or systems could be affected by the research?
  2. What consent, confidentiality, safeguarding, security or data-governance requirements apply?
  3. What is the residual risk after those controls, and why is it proportionate?
  4. How will you store, access, retain and dispose of research data under your institution's rules?
  5. Could the method, data or findings be misused, and how will you limit that risk?

4. Feasibility and project control

  1. What resources, permissions and expertise does the plan depend on?
  2. What is the critical path from approval to completion?
  3. What will you do if recruitment, access, equipment or data quality falls below the required level?
  4. Which milestones show that the project is on track?
  5. What is outside scope, and why is excluding it necessary?

5. Limits and follow-up

  1. What cannot be concluded from this design?
  2. Which alternative explanation would be hardest for the project to rule out?
  3. What would you revise if initial pilot evidence contradicts an assumption?
  4. How will your claim remain useful if the findings are mixed or null?
  5. What is the next responsible research step after this project?

Use an answer worksheet, not a script

For every question above, write five brief prompts rather than memorising a paragraph. This makes the answer adaptable when a committee changes the wording or asks a follow-up.

PromptWhat to write
ClaimYour direct answer in one sentence.
EvidenceThe source, preliminary observation, dataset or established constraint supporting it.
ReasoningWhy that evidence leads to the proposed design choice.
AlternativeThe strongest viable alternative and why it is less suitable for this question.
BoundaryThe limitation, risk or condition that limits the claim, plus the control or next action.

Question: Why interviews rather than a survey?

Answer trace: The study asks how first-time users interpret uncertainty warnings. Interview data can reveal their reasoning, while a survey would estimate predefined responses at a broader scale. The trade-off is that the study will not estimate prevalence; the proposal will make a bounded claim about the decision process and use a transparent sampling rationale.

Run a 30-minute question practice session

0–5 minutes: choose five questions covering each question family.

5–17 minutes: answer each aloud in two minutes; record only points that lack evidence, an alternative or a boundary.

17–25 minutes: ask one follow-up to each weak answer: “What would change your decision?” or “Why not the alternative?”

25–30 minutes: repair the three highest-leverage gaps in the proposal or answer notes, then schedule a retest.

Score observable answer quality: directness, evidence, method fit, limitation control and feasibility. Do not use a mock session to predict the formal outcome. Its purpose is to reduce uncertainty before the proposal is fixed.

Frequently asked questions

What questions are asked in a research proposal defense?

They commonly test the problem, literature gap, question-method fit, alternatives, evidence, ethics, feasibility, analysis and the limits of the expected contribution. Your programme determines the actual assessment scope.

How should I answer a proposal defense question?

Answer the decision behind the question: state the claim, give its evidence, explain the design or control, compare the strongest realistic alternative, and name the relevant limitation.

What if I do not know the answer?

State the part you can answer, separate it from what remains uncertain, and define the safe next action. Do not invent evidence or promise an unplanned change without considering its effects on the design.

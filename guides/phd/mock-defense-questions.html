Mock Defense Questions: 30 Questions to Practise

Mock defense questions should test the decisions behind your research, not only your ability to recite a prepared talk. A useful rehearsal asks why the problem matters, how the method fits the question, what the evidence can support, where the limitations sit and what you would change if a committee challenged an assumption.

This guide is for proposal, thesis and dissertation defense practice. If you first need the definition and purpose of a rehearsal, read what a mock defense is. If you are preparing a proposal specifically, use the research proposal defense questions guide.

Core idea: Do not practise a fixed script. Practise answer control: direct claim, supporting evidence, method logic, realistic alternative, limitation and next step.

1. What a mock defense question is testing

A formal defense is controlled by your institution, programme and committee. A mock defense has no decision-making authority. Its value is diagnostic: it reveals which parts of your reasoning become unclear when someone changes the wording, asks for evidence or follows a weak answer.

University preparation guidance points to the same practical pattern. The University of Central Florida's Writing Center advises students to review the thesis thoroughly, practise the presentation multiple times and use a mock defense to practise answering live questions. National University lists example oral defense questions on contribution, literature, theory, methods, surprises, limitations and future extension. Sacramento State's dissertation handbook highlights committee questions on literature choices, statistics, sample, theoretical base, scope and limitations. These sources are institution-specific examples, not universal rules.

Question type What it tests What a strong answer shows
Problem and contribution Whether the work matters. A specific gap, not a broad topic claim.
Method and evidence Whether the design supports the claim. Method fit, alternatives and trade-offs.
Findings or expected findings Whether you understand what the evidence can establish. Clear distinction between demonstrated, suggested and unknown.
Limitations and scope Whether you control the boundaries of the work. Limitations stated without defensiveness or overclaiming.
Next steps and implications Whether the research can be used responsibly. Practical or scholarly value with realistic conditions.

2. Questions about the research problem

  1. What problem does this research address, and why is it worth examining?
  2. What is the narrowest version of the gap you are claiming?
  3. Who is affected by this problem, uncertainty or decision?
  4. What would remain unclear if your study had not been done?
  5. Which part of the problem is outside your scope, and why?

Weak answer: This topic is important because many researchers are interested in it.

Stronger answer: The problem is not just that the topic is popular. The specific uncertainty is how first-year doctoral students interpret ambiguous supervisor feedback before deciding whether to revise their project. That decision point matters because unclear interpretation can delay progress or lead to unnecessary changes.

3. Questions about contribution and originality

  1. What is the main contribution of the work?
  2. Is the contribution empirical, methodological, conceptual, theoretical, practical or a combination?
  3. How does your work differ from the closest existing study?
  4. What is the strongest possible criticism of your contribution claim?
  5. Which claim should you make more cautiously?

In a mock session, press for precision. If the answer is “my work fills a gap”, ask: which gap, for whom, at what level of evidence, and compared with which closest alternative?

4. Questions about methods and evidence

  1. Why did you choose this method rather than the strongest realistic alternative?
  2. What evidence does the method produce, and what can it not produce?
  3. How did you choose the sample, cases, texts, dataset or materials?
  4. How does the analysis move from raw evidence to a defensible answer?
  5. What quality checks, validity checks or credibility checks support the interpretation?

Question: Why interviews rather than a survey?

Answer trace: Interviews fit because the study examines how participants reason through a decision. A survey would increase scale, but it would require predefined response categories and would not capture the reasoning sequence. The trade-off is that I can make a bounded claim about interpretation processes, not prevalence across the whole population.

5. Questions about findings, results or expected results

  1. What is the most important finding or expected finding?
  2. How does the evidence answer the research question?
  3. Which result was unexpected, ambiguous or difficult to interpret?
  4. What alternative explanation did you consider?
  5. What would count as evidence against your interpretation?

For a proposal defense, adapt these questions to expected evidence rather than final findings. For example: what kind of result would support the proposed claim, and what would force you to revise the design?

6. Questions about limitations and scope

  1. What is the most important limitation of the work?
  2. Which limitation affects interpretation but does not invalidate the study?
  3. What cannot be concluded from this design?
  4. What would you do differently if you repeated the study?
  5. Which question from the committee would be hardest to answer, and why?

A strong limitation answer has three parts: name the limitation, explain the boundary it creates and state how you controlled or reported it. Do not treat a limitation as a confession. Treat it as a boundary on the claim.

7. Questions about implications and next steps

  1. What should a researcher, practitioner or policy audience do differently because of this work?
  2. What is the safest next research step?
  3. How could someone build on the study without overstating it?
  4. What is the practical value of a mixed, null or partial result?
  5. How has conducting this research changed your judgement as a researcher?

The last question is useful because many defenses test ownership. The committee wants to hear that you understand not only the document, but the reasoning journey that produced it.

8. Use follow-up questions to make the rehearsal realistic

A weak mock defense uses only one question at a time. A realistic one follows the answer. After each response, ask one of these follow-ups:

  • What evidence supports that claim?
  • Why not the alternative method?
  • What would change your interpretation?
  • What is the boundary of that claim?
  • Where does the thesis or proposal show that?
  • What would you do if that assumption fails?
  • How would you explain that to someone outside your field?

These follow-ups prevent the session from becoming memorisation practice. They test whether the reasoning transfers when the committee asks the question in a different form.

9. A 45-minute mock defense plan

0-5 minutes: name the real assessment, time limit, committee expectations and one weak area to stress-test.

5-12 minutes: give a short presentation or thesis overview without reading a script.

12-30 minutes: answer 8 to 12 questions across problem, contribution, method, evidence, limitations and next steps.

30-38 minutes: receive feedback on answer clarity, evidence, method fit, limitation control and confidence under follow-up.

38-45 minutes: choose three repairs: one content repair, one answer repair and one presentation repair.

Use the system around you before paying for external support. Your supervisor, committee chair, research group, doctoral school, writing centre, methods adviser and recent graduates may already provide practice, examples or process knowledge. For example, attending another defense in your programme can show the local rhythm of presentation, committee questions and audience questions.

10. Answer framework for hard questions

Step What to say
Direct answer Answer the question in one sentence before giving background.
Evidence Name the source, result, method, observation or constraint that supports the answer.
Reasoning Explain how the evidence leads to your claim or design choice.
Alternative Compare the strongest realistic alternative rather than ignoring it.
Boundary State what the answer does not prove, and what you would do next.

Hard question: Why should we trust this interpretation?

Controlled answer: The interpretation is supported by three forms of evidence: repeated patterns across interviews, negative cases that helped refine the coding and alignment with the decision sequence described in the literature. It does not prove prevalence across the whole population. The claim is about how this sample reasoned through the decision, and I report the sampling boundary explicitly.

Frequently asked questions

What questions should I ask in a mock defense?

Ask questions that test problem, contribution, method, evidence, limitations, implications and next steps. Add follow-ups that ask for evidence, alternatives and boundaries.

How many mock defense questions should I practise?

A focused session can use 8 to 12 questions. The full bank of 30 helps you choose coverage, but the goal is to test every major research decision rather than rush through the whole list.

Should I know the questions in advance?

You can know the broad areas, but the exact wording and follow-ups should usually stay unseen. Otherwise the session tests memory more than reasoning.

What if I cannot answer?

Pause, separate what you know from what remains uncertain, identify the boundary of the work and define the safest next action. It is better to acknowledge a limit than to invent evidence.

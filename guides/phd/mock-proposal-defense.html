Mock Proposal Defense: A 60-Minute Rehearsal Plan

A useful mock proposal defense ends with a better research plan and a short list of repairs. Organize a small practice panel, rehearse within your programme's presentation limit, invite follow-up questions, and record which decisions you cannot yet justify. You do not need completed results to explain why a proposed study is worth doing and how it could work.

This guide helps you organize the session. For the underlying questions, use our proposal defense question bank; for slides, use the proposal presentation guide.

What is a mock proposal defense?

A mock proposal defense is a practice presentation and discussion of a planned research project before its formal assessment. This working definition follows the University of Saskatchewan's proposal preparation guidance, which recommends a rehearsal with invited participants to practise the presentation, technology and questions. A mock session gives feedback; it does not approve the proposal.

For example, a student planning interviews can rehearse why interviews fit the question, how recruitment will work, and what happens if access fails. They should distinguish planned analysis from actual findings.

Use your local rules first. The agenda below is a MockBase practice suggestion, not a university requirement or assessment rubric. Confirm your presentation time, panel arrangements, permitted materials and any adjustments with your programme.

1. Confirm the real format before inviting people

Ask your supervisor or graduate administrator for the current handbook, assessment criteria and instructions. Programme formats vary. For example, the University of Utah Robotics handbook describes a written proposal, public presentation and closed oral defense. Do not assume that sequence applies to your programme.

Make a one-paragraph briefing for the practice panel: the assessment you are preparing for, the allowed presentation time, whether questions interrupt the talk, and the two research decisions you most want tested. For example: “Please test whether my proposed data can answer the question and whether my recruitment fallback preserves the study's purpose.”

2. Borrow the support already around you

Start with your research group, doctoral peers and supervisor before buying external coaching. A recent candidate can explain the local process; a methods adviser can challenge the design; a writing centre can help clarify the argument. Check availability and avoid assuming that a supervisor can attend every rehearsal.

Two peers can combine these roles. If practising alone, deliver the talk aloud, draw unfamiliar questions from the question bank, and write down answers that rely on assumptions. Arrange a later peer session to test follow-ups you cannot anticipate yourself.

3. Send a small preparation pack

Give participants enough context to question the plan: your current slides, a one-page research summary and the local criteria you are allowed to share. Agree on reading time. The summary should identify the research question, closest relevant work, proposed evidence, analysis approach, dependencies and unresolved decisions.

For example, write “Access requested; not confirmed” beside a partner dataset. That lets a reviewer test the actual risk instead of treating access as settled. Share only materials participants are permitted to see; remove personal or confidential data from practice copies. Agree before recording a session and use your institution's approved arrangements where relevant.

4. Run a 60-minute session

This example reserves 15 minutes for a short presentation. If your real talk is longer, extend the session and preserve time for questions and feedback. The purpose is to rehearse your actual constraints.

Ask the observer to postpone routine presentation tips until feedback time. Constant interruptions to correct wording can prevent the candidate from practising the full sequence. Follow the real assessment's question rules where these are known.

5. Test decisions that matter before results exist

Choose a small set of questions with follow-ups. The panel should examine how the proposed work would produce a defensible answer, rather than asking the candidate to invent a successful outcome.

  • Contribution: What uncertainty would the study resolve? Follow-up: which closest study leaves that uncertainty open?
  • Design: Why does this method fit the question? Follow-up: what would the strongest alternative make easier or harder?
  • Analysis: How will you move from data to an answer? Follow-up: walk through one hypothetical example and label it as hypothetical.
  • Feasibility: Which dependency could prevent completion? Follow-up: what is the fallback, and does it change the question?
  • Interpretation: What if the evidence does not support your expectation? Follow-up: what could you still conclude?

Example challenge: “What if you cannot recruit through your partner organization?”

Example answer: “Partner access is not yet confirmed. I would check it at the agreed planning milestone and discuss an alternative recruitment route with my supervisor. That route could change the sample, so I would revise the scope of my claims and check whether approvals need updating before proceeding.”

The answer is useful because it identifies a dependency, a decision point and a consequence. Replace each part with your actual project details; a polished fictional contingency is not a workable plan.

6. Turn feedback into a reusable repair sheet

Copy the following columns into your own notes. Record what happened, rather than assigning a general confidence score. A fluent answer can still contain an unsupported claim.

Observed gapRepair and supportRetest evidence
Could not identify the closest comparison study.Check the literature with a subject librarian; add an explicit comparison.Explain the difference accurately without claiming nobody has studied the topic.
Named a method but skipped the analysis.Work through one illustrative data-to-answer example with methods support.Explain every step and its limits in response to a fresh question.

Add an owner, due date and status to each repair. Prioritize changes that affect whether the study can answer its question before cosmetic slide edits. For example, resolving an unavailable dataset matters more than replacing a slide icon.

7. Decide when to repeat, pause or seek advice

Repeat a focused segment when a repaired answer remains unclear under new wording. Pause repeated full rehearsals when the remaining problem needs evidence, access or supervisory advice: practising an unsupported answer again will not resolve its premise.

A useful stopping point for this practice cycle is that you can finish within the agreed time, justify the principal design choices, distinguish confirmed facts from plans, and name the remaining actions. These are preparation checks, not a prediction that you will pass. If a reviewer raises a fundamental design conflict, take it to your supervisor before rewriting the entire project.

Common practical questions

Do I need my actual committee at the mock session?

Not for the informal rehearsal described here. Peers can help you practise delivery and questioning. Ask your supervisor whether your programme has rules or expectations for a formal preparatory meeting.

Can I rehearse without finished slides?

Yes, for a reasoning check. Use a short outline to test the question, method and feasibility first. Run a later presentation rehearsal with the actual slides and equipment before the assessment.

Is a mock proposal defense the same as a mock thesis defense?

The practice structure may overlap, but the emphasis differs. A proposal rehearsal tests a planned study; a completed-thesis rehearsal examines work already done and the evidence supporting its contribution. Read the mock defense overview to choose the appropriate path.
